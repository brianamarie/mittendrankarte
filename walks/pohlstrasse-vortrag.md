# Pohlstraße: a twenty-minute walk

A draft to be spoken while walking west, from the Flottwellstraße to the Kluckstraße. About 2,400 words, a measured twenty minutes. The pin list it walks through is in `walks/pohlstrasse.md`.

---

We start here, at the east end, where the Pohlstraße meets the Flottwellstraße and the Dennewitzstraße. The U-Bahn is overhead. House numbers are still in single digits. The first pin on the map is Pohlstraße 4. If we keep walking west we cover about seven hundred metres, the numbers rise, and the last door is Pohlstraße 97, where the street meets the Kluckstraße. Twenty-six pins sit on that line. A hundred and nine articles are attached to them. Four of the pins are only the word Pohlstraße, set down on the roadway, and those four hold sixty-five of the articles. The doors are fewer, and they are the ones with a name on them.

Before any of those doors, the street has had three names, and the German gazetteers agree on the sequence.

From 10 June 1862 until 7 March 1936 this was the Steglitzer Straße. The Luisenstadt street page for the Steglitzer Straße says so, and it says the name referred to the village of Steglitz, written down in 1242, a place that is now an Ortsteil in Steglitz-Zehlendorf. On 7 March 1936 the street became the Ludendorffstraße, after Erich Ludendorff. He was the general of the First World War who later stood with the völkisch right, in the Kapp putsch of 1920 and the Hitler-Ludendorff putsch of 1923, and who died in Munich in 1937. Paul Enck wrote that renaming up for mitteNdran in October 2020, in „Straßen im Kiez: Pohlstraße“, and he set it next to the older Berlin habit of naming streets after generals. The Generalszug had been there since 1862. Julius Löwenberg, in a Berlin guide of 1850, had already called the mass of military street names a peculiarity of this city.

On 31 July 1947 the name changed again. The Berlin Lexikon of the Luisenstädtischer Bildungsverein dates the Pohlstraße from that day, and names the person: the Kommunalpolitikerin Ottilie Pohl. The same date is in the Verordnungsblatt für Groß-Berlin of 1947, page 218, in the list of streets renamed with the consent of the police president. In the Tiergarten block of that list, Ludendorffstraße becomes Pohlstraße, in the company of other names the Nazi years had put on this district. Kauperts, the street directory, has the same three names and the same woman, and dates the Pohlstraße from 1946. The ordinance is the date to keep. Kauperts is a year early.

That is as far as the name is settled. Why this particular street was called after Steglitz, in the same year as Hobrecht’s expansion plan, the gazetteers do not say. What stood on the ground before 10 June 1862, a path or a field or a surveyor’s line, is still to find. The Senatsverwaltung keeps the plan series, from the Hobrecht plan of 1862 through the Flächennutzungsplan of 1965. We have not yet laid a sheet of that series on this pavement.

The woman the street is named for lived her life in Moabit.

Ottilie Pohl was born Taube Levit on 14 November 1867 in Schönwalde, near Luckau in the Niederlausitz. Wikipedia, which now carries the family Paul Enck reconstructed for mitteNdran, gives her parents as Moritz Levit, a Jewish trader, and Friederike Zirker. Her mother died in 1885. Her father brought the children to Berlin. She learned the trade of a Putzmacherin, a milliner. She signed papers as Taube Pohl. In 1893, after her father’s death, she married Wilhelm Hermann Pohl, a coachman and later a factory worker. They had three children. Gertrud, born in 1895, died in 1946. Hans died at eight months. Fritz, born in 1900, lived until 1978, and from his marriage there is one child. The fates of several of her siblings are still marked unknown on that page. Max was murdered, as she was, in the camps. That gap is still a gap.

She joined the SPD after the Socialist Law lapsed. During the First World War she went to the USPD, distributed the Spartakusbriefe with her son, and in 1920 was elected to the Stadtverordnetenversammlung for Tiergarten. She sat on the poor-relief committee and the school committee. She was on the board of the Berlin consumers’ cooperative. Later she was in the KPD. In the BVG strike of 1932 she worked in the solidarity kitchen. After 1933 she worked in the Rote Hilfe, with Rosa Lindemann, in coffee circles in Moabit that looked after the children of people who had been arrested. Her last address was Beusselstraße 43. Kauperts says the Gestapo arrested her in August 1940, at seventy-three, and that on 20 November 1942 she was taken from the women’s prison on Barnimstraße with the 75th transport to Theresienstadt. Wikipedia says the 1940 sentence was eight months, served at the women’s prison on Kantstraße, that she was released at the end of 1941 and went on with the illegal work, and that the deportation in November 1942 followed her being Jewish. Both accounts put her death in Theresienstadt in December 1943. She was seventy-six. The prison, and the reason printed on the transport, are the part the two sources do not yet share. Hans-Peter Doege wrote a short life of her in the Berlinische Monatsschrift in 2000. Silva Wittfeld wrote her in *Aufbrüche. Frauengeschichte(n) aus Tiergarten*, in 1999. Luise Kraushaar’s *Deutsche Widerstandskämpfer* of 1970 gives her two pages. Enck’s point in the mitteNdran piece was that those pages had almost no family. The family is now on Wikipedia because he went looking under the name Taube.

The honour is in Moabit. A Berliner Gedenktafel hung at Beusselstraße 43 from 1989 until about 2017. On 10 May 2025 a Stolperstein was laid in front of that house. On this street her name is on the street sign. Enck walked it in 2020 and found no plaque. That is still the case. Someone in the Beusselstraße has the stone. Someone here has the name, and no sentence under it.

Under the name, the street has a few buildings the official lists will actually describe, and a long stretch they will not.

The Berlin Lexikon, after the renaming, has one sentence of fabric. The former Charlotten-Töchterschule at number 62, built in 1877 and 1878 by Hermann Blankenstein, is a listed monument. The Denkmaldatenbank of the Senatsverwaltung, object 09050200, puts that school together with the Falk-Realgymnasium on Lützowstraße 83 to 85. Blankenstein and Adolf Reich designed it for the city. The classrooms of the two schools run one behind the other, almost ninety metres, with a teacher’s house on the Pohlstraße as the way in to the girls’ school. That house is yellow brick with red terracotta, and it is how the schools show themselves to this street. The complex was modernised between 1988 and 1991. The block opposite, the database says, stayed empty after the war, so the gable is visible. That is the one war sentence the monument entry gives us.

The other school is one the archive has had to locate twice. The Körner-Realschule began in 1887 as the III. Höhere Bürgerschule, in temporary rooms, and in autumn 1891 moved into a new building at Steglitzer Straße 8a, reached by a passage between houses 8 and 9. Today that is Pohlstraße 18. In 1912, at the twenty-fifth anniversary, it was named for Theodor Körner, the poet of the wars of 1813, who had already given his name to the Körnerstraße around the corner. A postcard of 1915, which Ralf Schmiedecke gave to mitteNdran, shows the gate and the words „III. Höhere Bürgerschule“ over it, and the street running west toward the bend at the Potsdamer Straße, lined with the neobaroque fronts of about 1870. Those fronts stood until the war. Which houses fell, and which of today’s doors are new, is not in the street lexicon. The postcard is the picture. A damage map for this street is one of the things we still need.

The railway is documented by a plaque. In October 1926 the new U-Bahn line opened from Gleisdreieck down to the underground station at Kurfürstenstraße, a drop of nearly twenty metres. Between the Kurfürstenstraße and the Pohlstraße the ramp runs through a block of houses. The Gedenktafel „Viadukt im Häuserblock“, at Kurfürstenstraße and Dennewitzstraße, says thirty plots were bought for it, wholly or in part, and that the tracks sit in a double-walled tube meant to keep the noise down. It is not quiet. The studios in the arches at Pohlstraße 11 are the rooms that occupation left behind. Five of our articles sit on that door: youth work under the arches, and the Gallery Weekend openings of the ateliers.

There is one claim I would keep in the talk as a claim, and go and check. In an rbb piece on the Stadtteilverein, three facts are given for this street. The second is Ottilie Pohl. The third is that the street runs parallel to the Kurfürstenstraße. The first is that the Pohlstraße was part of the West Berlin motorway plans of 1965, a line from the Urania through the Kiez toward Kreuzberg, never built, because of protest. The motorway the newspapers and the Bürgerinitiative actually document is the Westtangente: opened in 1968 from Steglitz to the Schöneberger Kreuz, then planned north along the Wannsee railway and in a tunnel under the Tiergarten. The Tagesspiegel, the BUND, and the chronicle of the BI Westtangente all describe that line. It is a different line from the one rbb draws through this Kiez. Until we put the Flächennutzungsplan sheet of 1965 on the Pohlstraße, that sentence stays a sentence from a broadcast.

From here the walk is the pins, in the order the street offers them.

Pohlstraße 4, the first pin, holds one article, a walk into the past about ENIGMA in the Steglitzer Straße. The old name is doing the work in that title. Number 7 is an afternoon of tea with the neighbours. Number 8 is the missing football cage, and a conversation with the Bezirksstadtrat Ephraim Gothe. Number 11 is the arches, five articles, the studios that open for the Gallery Weekend. Number 9 to 16 is a single pin for a stretch of houses, one article, on the disappearance of protected open space.

Then the school block. Number 16 asks where the Körner-Realschule was. Number 18 answers with the school itself. Number 20 asks again, and the answer is the passage between the old numbers 8 and 9, behind today’s number 20. Number 23 is a Kiez walk with the two Stadträte, Almut Neumann and Ephraim Gothe. Number 37 is a shop, Ivy and Gault, ceramics and a Korean kitchen.

Past number 37 the doors stop for a while and the street pin begins. At 52.50044, 13.36665, twenty-two articles share one dot. Some of them are the street: the little fences, the unsealing of the pavements that was supposed to stay, the Spielstraße days of 2025 and 2026, the path through to the Kurfürstenstraße. Others are passing through: the new steel viaduct of the U1, a walk around the Kiezblock Flottwell, the Berlin Art Week, a building site at Gleisdreieck, Möbel Hübner closing after 115 years, Walter Benjamin in the Blumeshof, the cholera of 1831. They mention the Pohlstraße. Their subject is elsewhere. A presentation can say that in one sentence and keep walking. Opening all twenty-two is a second evening.

A few metres on, at 52.50060, 13.36527, is the main street pin, the one set in the middle of the roadway. Thirty-five articles. This is where the Spielstraße lives in the archive: the announcements, the mornings after, the puppet theatre, the Pohlonaise. It is where the greening lives: the start of the unsealing on Pohlstraße and Kluckstraße, the tree pits, the plants, the argument about bicycle streets, and the piece that calls the unsealing of the pavements a farce. It is where „Kiez mit Kind“ puts the secret playground at the upper end of the street. And it is where a great many other pieces have been given this coordinate because the text says Pohlstraße once: a Herzog exhibition at the Kinemathek, an issue of the magazine, a parcel locker, the Falk-Realgymnasium, the parish schools, a café at a mill, the Ledermann family, a press view of the neighbourhood. The pin is honest about the street. It is a poor guide to which sentence in the article the street appears in. That sorting is the work after this walk.

Number 44 is one article, photographs of the Pohlstraße between 1970 and 2025. That pin is the closest thing we have, in the archive, to the missing damage map: a before and after, taken from the street itself.

Then a single press roundup on a street pin of its own. Then number 58, the history of the shop Möbel Hübner. Then number 65, Isotop playing as a trio. Then another shared street pin, seven articles, further west: the puppet theatre again, new plants for Pohlstraße and Kluckstraße, the last Spielstraße of the year, the wild nineteen-twenties in Tiergarten-Süd, a neighbourhood clean-up.

Number 69 is parcels and returns. Number 71 is a gallery door: Uschi Krempel, Ophelia Beckmann, a vernissage, a photography show, and the Gallery Weekend of 2026, when the smaller galleries at the edge of the Kiez are the subject. Number 60 is the Stolpersteine for Ossip and Gertrud Schnirlin, laid by Gunter Demnig, and a third article that landed on the same door. A full list of every Stolperstein on this street, from the Stolperstein project, is still to pull. We know 60. We are about to pass 64.

Number 62 is the Charlottenschule article, the girls’ school the monument list describes from the other direction, as architecture. Number 75 is Les Climats, the wine bar, the jazz evening of May 2023, the Gründonnerstag menu, and the Pohlonaise invitations that use this door as their start. One more past-walk, on the girls of the Charlottenschule, is pinned here too. Number 62 to 64 holds a single piece on strong women of the Potsdamer Straße. Number 64 is the Kubatzky family: the laying of the stones, the stones themselves, a Gallery Weekend note, and a neighbourhood roundup.

Number 89, close to the Kluckstraße, is forty years of the Stadtteilverein Tiergarten. The rbb piece that gave us the motorway sentence was filmed along this street because the Verein’s story runs along it. The Verein’s office is now in the Lützowstraße. The pin is here.

Number 97 is the last pin, at 52.50186, 13.35966. Two articles. One is a walk into the past about Minna Lehmann at this address. One is a press roundup that names the same house. The Kluckstraße is the end of the roadway. The upper end of the Pohlstraße, the end the playground article calls the upper end, is this end.

What we can say at this corner, having walked it once, is short.

The name is documented. Steglitzer Straße from 1862, Ludendorffstraße from March 1936, Pohlstraße from 31 July 1947, after a woman who lived and was killed in another part of the district. The ordinance, the Luisenstadt lexicon, and Wikipedia’s life of her are enough for that opening. Kauperts is the directory to cite and then correct by a year. The building we can point at is the teacher’s house of the Charlotten-Töchterschule at number 62, and the gate of the Körner-Realschule, which the postcard still shows and the street no longer does. The railway of 1926 is the reason number 11 is a row of arches.

What we cannot yet say is why the street was Steglitz’s, what the Hobrecht sheet drew here, which houses the war took, whether a motorway line was ever inked along this pavement, and how many Stolpersteine the street holds beyond the Schnirlins and the Kubatzkys. The address books for 1890, 1910 and 1930 would tell us who kept a shop. They are not in the street pages we have.

The articles are in three kinds. Doors, where a house number is the subject. The street itself, where the Spielstraße, the pavements and the playground are the subject, and they deserve a pin on the roadway. And the pile in the middle, where the Pohlstraße is a sentence on the way to somewhere else. The next hour is that pile, one article at a time, starting with the twenty-two on the eastern street pin and the thirty-five on the middle one.

We are at the Kluckstraße. That is the end of this street.
